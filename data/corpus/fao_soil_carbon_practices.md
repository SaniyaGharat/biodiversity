# Section: Cover Cropping and SOC Sequestration Dynamics

Cover cropping represents one of the most effective agroecological interventions for enhancing soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks, preventing soil erosion, and enhancing microbial biomass. Incorporating cover crops during fallow or inter-cropping periods supplies continuous fresh organic matter to the soil profile through both aboveground residue and extensive root exudates.

According to global meta-analyses synthesized by the FAO, continuous winter or seasonal cover cropping increases topsoil (0-30 cm) organic carbon sequestration rates by an average of 0.32 to 0.55 tonnes of Carbon per hectare per year (t C/ha/yr). Leguminous cover crops (such as hairy vetch, crimson clover, and sunn hemp) fix atmospheric nitrogen, enhancing the microbial C:N ratio and accelerating the stabilization of carbon in mineral-associated organic matter (MAOM).

In contrast, non-leguminous cover crops (e.g., cereal rye, radishes, and sudangrass) produce high carbon-to-nitrogen biomass, which improves soil aggregate stability, increases water retention capacity by up to 20-35%, and mitigates nitrate leaching into groundwater aquifers by over 40%.

# Section: Conservation Tillage and Crop Residue Retention

Zero-tillage (no-till) coupled with direct crop residue retention (retaining at least 30% ground cover) reduces oxidative loss of soil carbon caused by physical disruption of soil aggregates. Physical soil disruption in intensive tillage systems exposes previously protected organic matter within macroaggregates to microbial oxidation, leading to rapid carbon mineralization and CO2 emission.

When conservation tillage is combined with diversified crop rotations, the accumulation of particulate organic carbon (POC) in the upper 0-15 cm layer creates a buffered microclimate, reducing topsoil temperature fluctuations and decreasing evaporative water loss. Furthermore, cation exchange capacity (CEC) increases proportionally with organic matter accumulation, thereby improving the nutrient-supplying power of agricultural soils.

# Section: Soil Acidity, Alkalinity and pH Buffering

Soil pH (measured in H2O solution, phh2o) strongly dictates microbial community composition and enzymatic decomposition rates. In strongly acidic soils (pH < 5.5), aluminum and manganese toxicity inhibit root elongation, reducing root carbon inputs and constraining nitrogen fixation by rhizobia. In alkaline and calcareous soils (pH > 8.0), phosphorus availability is restricted due to calcium phosphate precipitation.

Restoring degraded soils through organic amendments and cover cropping promotes biological buffering capacity, mitigating rapid acidification and enhancing soil microbial diversity.
